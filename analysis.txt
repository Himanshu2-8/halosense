PS1 — The Silent Co-Driver (audio → stress)

Pipeline: audio → VAD/denoise → Whisper (transcript + word timestamps) → two parallel heads → fusion → overlay on lap times.

HF assets that make this cheap:
- ASR: openai/whisper-small (or distil-whisper/distil-small.en for speed). CPU-fine on 5–15s clips.
- The good one: audeering/wav2vec2-large-robust-12-ft-emotion-msp-dim — outputs continuous arousal / dominance / valence instead of acted-emotion classes. This maps onto Russell's circumplex and gives you a defensible, research-grounded label rule:
  - high arousal + low valence → Stressed
  - low arousal + neutral valence → Calm
  - low arousal + low valence → Tired
- Text head on the transcript: facebook/bart-large-mnli zero-shot over racing-specific intents ("complaining about tyres", "requesting strategy change", "acknowledging"), or cardiffnlp/twitter-roberta-base-sentiment-latest.
- Alternative SER baselines to compare against: superb/wav2vec2-base-superb-er, ehcalabres/wav2vec2-lg-xlsr-en-speech-emotion-recognition (RAVDESS).

Lap-time data is a solved problem. fastf1 gives you real lap times, sector times, stint, tyre compound, and weather for any session since 2018, cached locally, free. This half of the app is essentially free accuracy.

Where the real risks are:

- Domain mismatch is the #1 risk. Every SER model on the Hub was trained on acted studio speech (RAVDESS) or clean call-center audio. F1 radio is heavily compressed, clipped, engine-saturated, and often shouted. Expect mediocre raw accuracy. Mitigation: present arousal/valence as a continuous signal with confidence, not a hard verdict — and be upfront about it in the pitch. Judges respect a stated limitation far more than an overclaimed 95%.
- "Tired" doesn't exist in any SER label set. You have to derive it. This is your differentiator: Whisper gives word-level timestamps → compute speech rate (words/sec), pause ratio, mean pause length, pitch decline. Fatigue = low arousal + slowed speech + long pauses. Cheap to compute, genuinely novel, and it's the kind of thing that makes a judge sit up.
- Whisper hallucinates on silence/noise. Gate it with VAD (silero-vad, or pyannote/voice-activity-detection) and set no_speech_threshold.
- No ground truth exists for stress labels. Mitigation: hand-label 25–30 clips yourself as a mini eval set and report agreement + a confusion matrix. That converts a weakness into visible "Research" credit.

Data plan (~1–2 hours): Grab 25–40 team radio clips from YouTube compilations — the iconic ones are perfect because they're emotionally unambiguous and the judges will recognise them ("Bono, my tyres are gone", Kimi's "leave me alone", Verstappen's rants, Alonso's "I don't want that"). Hand-write a metadata.csv: clip, driver, race, lap. Then FastF1 fills in the lap times automatically. Note: F1's live-timing static endpoint exposes a TeamRadio.json with mp3 URLs which community scripts use — worth 15 minutes of investigation, but don't let it block you; YouTube + ffmpeg is the safe path.

Demo strength: high. Audio is visceral. Press play, the waveform runs, the UI lights up STRESSED · arousal 0.87, and the lap chart next to it shows a 1.2s dropoff on that exact lap. That's a 45-second demo people remember.

PS2 — Weather Whiplash (image → track condition)

HF assets:
- Zero-shot baseline: openai/clip-vit-base-patch32 or google/siglip-base-patch16-224 with prompts like "a dry race track" / "a wet race track with standing water". Works decently out of the box.
- But zero-shot alone violates Rule 2. The right move: extract CLIP/SigLIP embeddings for ~300–600 labeled frames and train a linear probe / small MLP head on top. Minutes of compute, big accuracy jump, and it's unambiguously your model. Alternative: fine-tune google/vit-base-patch16-224 or a timm MobileNetV3 — 10–20 min on a Colab T4.

The deep problem nobody will notice until day 3:

The statement asks you to label a single image as Dry / Damp / Wet / **Drying**. "Drying" is not a property of an image. A damp track and a drying track are pixel-for-pixel identical in one frame — drying is a temporal property. A 4-class single-frame classifier will confuse Damp↔Drying forever and your demo will look broken.

The correct architecture, which is also your winning differentiator: have the model predict a continuous wetness score (or just 3 static classes Dry/Damp/Wet), then derive Drying vs Getting wetter from the slope of that score over the frame sequence. This:
- fixes the ill-posedness,
- directly satisfies "shows a simple trend: is the track getting better or worse",
- is your tire-change trigger (wetness crossing a threshold with negative slope = "crossover window approaching").

Most teams will train the naive 4-class model. Getting this right is the single biggest lever on PS2.

Other risks:
- Night races destroy naive classifiers. Wet tarmac under floodlights and dry tarmac under floodlights both look like a mirror. You must deliberately include night-dry frames in training or you'll ship a model that thinks Singapore is always monsoon.
- Onboard cameras: raindrops on the lens ≠ wet track. Related but distinct signal.
- Frames from one race are highly correlated — 1000 frames from Spa is not 1000 samples. You need diversity across tracks, angles, and lighting, which is what makes the labeling grind real (budget 3–4 hours).
- Good auxiliary features that are basically free: visible spray behind cars (strong wet signal), dark drying racing line vs uniformly dark track (the classic drying tell), specular puddle highlights.

Data shortcut worth knowing: FastF1's session.weather_data has a Rainfall boolean per minute. Pick races with known phase timelines (Turkey 2020 — wet throughout; Suzuka 2022 — wet→drying; Silverstone 2020 — dry; Singapore — night dry) and label by segment rather than per-frame. Cuts your labeling time by most of it.

Demo strength: medium-high. Clean and instantly legible — a wetness line trending down with a highlighted "TIRE CHANGE WINDOW" band is nice. But it's less striking than audio, and image classification demos are familiar to everyone.

Uniqueness: this is the lowest of the three. It's the easiest statement to imagine building, so I'd expect it to be the most-picked. That means polish is your only edge.

My recommendation: PS1, and it's not close given 4 days

The deciding argument isn't that PS1 is a better idea — it's that PS1 requires zero model training and its numeric half is free. That buys you three full days for the frontend, the connected end-to-end flow, and the iteration pass — which is precisely the list the organisers said they reward. PS2 spends those days on labeling and training, and you arrive at Aug 15 with a working model behind a rushed UI.

Secondary: PS1 lets you stack four models (ASR + prosody + text sentiment + lap correlation), so there's visibly more AI per minute of demo. And the field's PS1 submissions will overwhelmingly be Whisper + a RAVDESS classifier — the arousal/valence mapping plus derived fatigue features from speech rate and pauses clears that bar comfortably.

The honest case for PS2: it gives you a real, defensible accuracy number and a confusion matrix. With PS1 you'll be defending subjective labels with no ground truth, and a skeptical judge can push on that. If you have a genuine CV person on the team and reliable GPU access, PS2 is legitimate — just commit to the slope-based "Drying" architecture on day 1.

Decision rule: no GPU or no CV specialist → PS1. CV specialist + GPU + you want a hard metric → PS2.

One tactical tip for either: deploy on a Hugging Face Space (Gradio or Streamlit, or Docker with FastAPI + a React front end). Free CPU tier, gives you a public URL for the qualifier submission, and it satisfies Rule 3 on its own terms.

The two things that actually win you points

Both are cheap and both are in Lane A:

1. Arousal/valence instead of emotion classes. Every other PS1 team ships Whisper + a RAVDESS classifier. Framing stress as a continuous arousal/valence signal (Russell's circumplex) is defensible research, not a vibe.
2. Derived fatigue. "Tired" is in no SER model's label set — you compute it from Whisper's word timestamps: slowed speech rate + long pauses + low arousal. It's maybe 30 lines of code and it's the answer to the judge's inevitable "how do you detect tiredness?"
